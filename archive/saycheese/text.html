Say Cheese is an experimental process-based 'one at a time' live interaction between the artist's alter ego, Marcus Fisher, and participants. The interactions take place in bedrooms around the world - curator's own bedrooms, hotel bedrooms and constructed bedrooms within a gallery setting.

Say Cheese evolved organically. This text has been written in retrospect, and it attempts to contextualise the interactions and say something about the methodology that has been used.

Structure

Marcus positions himself on one side of a double bed whilst participants enter the bedroom one at a time for a short duration of 3-10 minutes. As participant walks into the bedroom, they see Marcus in a still position or in the middle of an activity, like reading or doing up his shoelaces. Marcus invites participant to the bed. Participants either sit or lay next to him. Opposite the bed a stills camera is positioned with a long shutter-release cable. At any chosen time the participant can press the shutter release and take photos. After a period of about a month each participant receives a letter and a photo of themselves with Marcus on the bed. Some participants respond with an email feedback, and occasionally verbally.

In some cities, as well as a stills camera, video was also used to document interactions. Participants are asked in advance if the photographs/videos can be shown publicly. Usually the participants are strangers, but occasionally they know the artist beforehand. Marcus facilitates any kind of interaction - physical, psychological, performative or at times non-interaction. Since the second interaction in Berlin Marcus might introduce a set of 'diagrams' into the interaction, where the participant is asked to choose one. The diagrams are simple line drawings suggesting possible bed positions for Marcus and participant to take on the bed. Each drawing is titled with a related opening sentence to be voiced by either participant or Marcus.

This type of interaction based on a diagram is similar to theatrical/cinematic improvisation, role-playing or Gestalt whereby Marcus and participant are devising and improvising a scene collaboratively. Marcus will lead or activate each interaction to varying degrees from 0-10 depending on participant, the energy in the room and his own mood. However, Marcus is always in control of the piece (in terms of professional responsibility) - apart from one time when his performative loss of temper with a participant was real. Marcus does anything he is asked to do by participant, be it of a sexual or spoken nature, apart from inflicting pain on himself or participant. Marcus never instigates physical contact with participant without asking first.

Props/toys/ambience
Depending on the location, time and the bedroom itself, props vary. Each bedroom as a 'set' or a backdrop will only subtly reflect Marcus at the time in an attempt to avoid possible theatrical clichés like a messy bachelor, or introverted scholar. The bedroom feels very private and used whilst at the same time indicates a 'lack of character' on Marcus's behalf. Generally props will include a local daily newspaper, alcohol, water, glasses for Marcus and participants, cigarettes and ashtray. These allow Marcus to be hospitable and provide an 'ice-breaker'.

In different places props/toys have also included a dildo, books, flowers, monitor with film playing (Hiroshima Môn amour) or TV channel, valentine card (valentine day in Bristol), personal belongings like laundry bags, towels, slippers, personal photographs on the wall, music; love songs or blues, rubbish on floor; coke cans, chocolate rappers, image on wall - a snapshot from the gulf war -a child in gas mask holds a kitten without one, pet without protection. Sometimes participants will bring small presents, which will stay on the bed.

Waiting Room
The project is advertised through mailing lists or mass media publicity. Participants book a time slot in advance. Numbers are limited. Participants meet each other in the waiting room; this can be the reception area in the hotel or another room in a domestic space. Participants might spend up to two hours with each other in the waiting room talking and drinking. After their interaction has taken place the participants either leave, or are encouraged not to talk about what had happened in the bedroom to other participants.

Usher

A performative usher takes one participant at a time and leaves them just outside the bedroom. After few minutes, the usher knocks on bedroom door as a sign for participant to leave. Some participants don't leave immediately and the usher has to knock on the door again after a while. The usher always sits outside the bedroom door for safety reasons.

Outcomes of the Interactions
The photographs create an instant fictional love narrative that may or may not have taken place. Some participants want to act out a particular 'pose' or fantasy, some want to be directed. Some use the diagrams for the body/bed position and the designated first sentence. Some interactions are warm, some challenging, some confessional, and some funny, some surreal, some kinky, some physical, some 'real', some explicitly sexual and some a little awkward.

There is no agenda concerning what kind of photos/encounters takes place, it is only important is to meet each participant on their own terms.

There are at least three performative spaces that take place during interactions. One is the 'theatrical' space where roles are played. During these types of interactions Marcus identity usually remains unquestionable and taken for granted in order to keep 'playing' or 'acting'. One participant left the room still 'acting' saying: 'I don't love you any more and I'm leaving now,' she walked out and slammed the door.

Another performative space is 'real' space where participants either relate to Marcus as 'oreet' or the 'artist', they ask about the project, the reasons for it, what other participants did, who is Marcus, in a way these interactions are kept within the 'real' and involve an exchange of information about the project without completely entering the performance space.

The last, which is perhaps the most interesting one, is the one that combines the two. Where the sense of the 'real' and the performative are completely mixed or there is a movement from one to the other. It is a very interesting space. For example when one participant said that our relationship was over (role playing) and the dialogue developed in that fictional direction for a while there was a sense in the room of an end of a long relationship. When Marcus asked will I see you again and the participant said probably not, there was a feeling of mourning for the fictional relationship mixed with a realistic acknowledgment of the 'real' improbability of that meeting.

Concepts/influences/questions/thoughts
As the title suggests 'Say Cheese' is based around the ritual of smiling to the camera and thus fixing a precious moment in time.

The photographs arrive about a month after the event. Has the participant forgot all about it? Does the photo give a different insight into the piece? What do participants do with the photos - throw them away? Cherish them? It is probably going to be the only photograph that participants will have with an orthodox Jew, especially in bed, at the same time it is probably one of hundreds of photographs the participants will have of themselves in any given situation. Is Marcus the native, an endangered species, a local bobby or a punk? What about other, public audiences who view the photographs? How much do the photographs tell of what actually took place? Are the photographs making the interactions look like 'more' had happened or 'less'? Why is the narrative absent leaving just a visual image?

'Say Cheese' was initially conceived with the idea that participants explore what possible fantasies they might want to act out for the camera and themselves in bed with an orthodox Jew. What kind of fetishes might such culture/religion might produce? 'Say cheese' was conceived as an experimental chamber to try out one's potential relationship to the 'Jew'. In this way the interaction can be used as a mirror, which participants can use to see themselves as they are relating to the Other. However the Other is complicated in that it is a simulation and a hybrid. Participants firstly have to identify for themselves who is the Other; the artist, the orthodox Jew, or the combination of both and more. Can Marcus, being the fairly even-tempered guy he is, be truly qualified to be named the Other, or do the participants, some of whom are wild and enterprising have better qualifications?

The power relationship in the interactions is fluid and complex, Marcus appears fairly passive to allow participants to act out their fantasies, still he is in control of the piece as the artist. If some of the interactions are to be viewed as potential theatrical or cinematic scripts, are they an equal collaboration? What states of 'self' or identity come into place in the interaction? Whilst Marcus is simulating a 'Jew' for some participants the experience of meeting Marcus is as good as meeting the 'real' thing. Is the artist's background and her relationships to Marcus creating something, which is as 'near to the real thing' as one might hope for under the circumstances? And what about the Jewish thing? For some participants Marcus represents 'the Jew' that which works as a cultural signifier, therefore any 'issues' be it guilt, perplexity, curiosity, or fetish they have with Judaism they can 'try out' with Marcus. Is the individual ethnic origin always also a signifier? Marcus has no character; he is a fiction without an history without a true mummy or a daddy. When met with a participant there is not much of 'him' that comes out, because there is no him. He has no obvious mannerism, voice or attitude, he refuses to act or 'be in character', he is only dressed up. Further more, his room which reflects his character is either completely indistinct or changes from venue to venue. There is no illusion. Yet there is. Our contemporary culture is well trained in reading simulacra and we don't need much. Marcus's 'lack of character' or 'personality' taps into a generalised, removed notion of what an orthodox Jew might be when no real contact takes place. He is just the guy next door.

Is Marcus political? Marcus has no political views. He is political and socially engaged art.

When say cheese becomes public.

'Say Cheese' is inspired by reality TV shows like 'Big brother', exploiting the rifling economy of the mini celebrity. The interactions are private, however the photographs, emails, videos are then made public. At the Liverpool Biennial the interactions were documented on video and shown the next day. Some viewers sat and watched three hours footage of the interactions from the previous day. To a large extent this consists of the same fascination of watching the utterly mundane life of the 'Big Brother' housemates for days. The desire to know how somebody else does it, the desire to have an insight into how somebody else 'does intimacy', how somebody else creates meaning with a stranger. What fiction another human being creates.

Without a doubt, apart from this general peephole fascination, viewers are also interested in the specific fictions that develop in the interactions. These fictions are very specific and particular to "Say Cheese' and to the encounter with Marcus.

Photographs and videos
'Say Cheese' also explores the photographs and the videos as a genre in themselves and the ongoing dialogue between performance and its documentation. 'Say Cheese' is most definitely aspiring to create photos that exist as visual images in their own right. The video work suggests potentials for short cinematic moments. The performance ultimately is not only of the moment; it is also acted for the camera. The camera being the only viewer in the room and the mediator of future viewers. How would the interaction exist without the viewer, without the camera? Will the moment be more precious since it has no material residues, only in the memory of the participants and Marcus, or will it become meaningless since there are no witness and no after life?

This project, '7 Acts of Love' online, is looking to resurrect the text, the narrative, the story, the fiction in language.
